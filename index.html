I'll Host It Myself

How A Dead Bank Card, A 4GB Laptop, And DuckDNS Got Me Back To Linux Administration .ep2|

Author: TheVimVictim . . . Date: 14 Sep 2026 . . . Est Time: 5min

I don't know how to start a blog post...My english teachers would mad at me right now! Let me get started...

SUMMARY: I'm renting a small Virtual Private Server that has been sitting idle for a while now, so I decided: why not just host my blog there? First this blog site, and later, my portfolio and hopefully my clients projects too.

Thinking back, I remember this one time as a student when I wanted my backend service hosted by AWS or Azure, I was looking for those free tiers...I couldn't create an account because my bank card had expired (and I wasn't smart enough to know virtual cards existed).

I was absolutely gutted by the fact that I could not create an account!!!

I was fuming because I desperately needed my application up and running to demo to potential funders (yes, I was delusional like that...way too much confidence on my To-Do API)

After a little bit of research, I learned I don't actually need to rent space from Big Tech to keep my applications online; I could use my own hardware for free. I started aspiring to host everything on my own machines. The biggest problem at the time was my tiny 4GB RAM laptop, which didn't have enough power to even open Chrome, I had to use Chromium. Oh!! Poverty... when I catch you!!.

Needless to say(I have always wanted to say that), my tiny machine stood zero chance at hosting an application backend. Luckily, an old ThinkPad (still 4GB RAM, lol) was lying around...So, I dedicated it entirely to hosting. I tried installing linux-server on it, but it totally kicked my butt, so I humbled myself and went back to Ubuntu. In that process, I learned a ton about Linux administration, a bit of Nginx, and the basics of cybersecurity.

I fell in love with that version of myself, but shortly into that era, I landed an internship. I had to focus in software development side of things and naturally I got consumed by writing code and slowly abandoned my love for system administration and information security.

One thing that stayed with me, though, was my love for free software and that lingering "I will do it, with no Big Tech" mindset. Wherever I go now, I try to convince people to switch to solid open source alternatives (within reason, of course). I coded away until it eventually got a bit boring, and I started doing actual human to human work. I am currently enjoying it :)

Through working with humans, my love for SysAdmin and Info Security came roaring back, mixed with love for DevOps. I'm boosting that passion by migrating this website from GitHub Pages to self-hosting.

I'm renting a small Private Server and using it as my ultimate playground. Today, I installed firewall rules, created admin users, and did all the housekeeping you have to do on a fresh system. It's a Linux machine, of course.

I threw a tiny webpage in there and spun up Nginx as my webserver...BOOM!!! I ran straight into an old problem of mindset: I refuse to hand out my raw server IP address to people, but I don't have a proper custom domain either. Side note: sending people raw IP addresses is just messy...I do not like it

So, I headed over to DuckDNS to get a free domain and pointed it to my server's IP. That kicked off a fun little troubleshooting session:

  • At first, I kept hitting connection timeout errors.
  • Then I realized I was pointing the DuckDNS record to my local IP instead of the server IP duh.
  • So I tried feeding DuckDNS my server's IPv4 address, but it wasn't having it.
  • That led me down the rabbit hole of discovering I needed an IPv6 address (and learning the hard way that on DigitalOcean, you actually have to power down the droplet first just to toggle IPv6 on).
  • LOL. I honestly thought I could just magically map IPv4 to IPv6, like there's a neat little web form somewhere where you paste an IPv4 address and out pops an IPv6 twin. Turns out, life doesn't work that way. That is why I am not a Network Technician.
  • Once I got the DNS sorted, I hit a very annoying rookie error: I accidentally pasted a whole URL into the Nginx config file instead of just the clean domain name for the server_name directive.

After fighting the webserver configurations and DNS problems, I have decided to call it a night...I will be back tomorrow with wisdom. Currently, I can't reach my webpage from my domain...Maar, tomorrow I am going to figure it out.

Feels good to be back in the terminal.

Do Not Be Like Bob

Why I Think SMS OTPs Are Easier To Hack Than You Think .ep1|

Author: TheVimVictim . . . Date: 28 Aug 2025 . . . Est Time: 4min

In this blog, imagine I am the hacker and Mr. Bob is the victim. Bob banks with PhishBank, a new bank in town. Bob loves PhishBank because of their excellent online services. Bob has a strong password and he knows not to share it with anyone because Bob has a lot of money in his account.

Since PhishBank is new, they roll out constant updates. So, they send Bob emails about new features and upgrades all the time. Bob does not mind these emails because Bob is in love with this bank.

Now, my problem is... I want Bob's money, all of it. But I know Bob's password is strong and very hard to guess and I know PhishBank sends SMS One-Time Password (OTP) to their customers as a security measure. Now, I have to find a way to get his password and then the OTP. Tough times!

So, I got this idea: Trick Bob into giving the credentials directly to me. I register a domain name that looks almost like phishbank.com (something like phishbank.co or ph1shbank.com). Then, I send him an email:

"Good day Mr. Bob, we have a new update on the system. Please log in within the next hour to avoid losing access. Click here to log in."

Bob panics. Afraid of losing his money, he rushes to the provided login link, enters his username and password. Seconds later, he receives an OTP SMS from the real PhishBank. He types it in. Lovely, Bob gets a thumbs up message on his screen.

Thank you Mr Bob, you have access.

Everything looks normal… or does it?

Here's the truth: Bob was never on the real site. He handed his login credentials directly to me.

Here's how I did it: I created a fake login page that looks exactly like the real PhishBank's. My fake login page captured his details and stored them in my database. A script I wrote forwarded the credentials to the real PhishBank system, that way, Bob still gets his OTP which he will then give to me.

And just like that…
I AM IN!!!

  • Don't click suspicious links – Go learn about URL structures. Worth 2min of your time.
  • Verify sender email/number – Go learn about domains and their standards.
  • Look for HTTPS – But remember, HTTPS alone doesn't mean it's safe.
  • Check grammar and urgency tactics – "Act now or lose access!" is a red flag.
  • Call the bank if unsure – Use the number on the back of your card, not the one in the email.
  • Report suspicious communications – Forward phishing emails/SMS to the bank's fraud team.
THIS POST IS BASED ON A LAB I USE TO EDUCATE MY FRIENDS AND FAMILY ABOUT THE DANGERS OF INSECURE SOFTWARE SYSTEMS, Bob AND PhishBank ARE FICTIONAL CHARACTERS. HACKING A SYSTEM WITHOUT PERMISSION FROM THE SYSTEM'S OWNER IS A VERY SERIOUS AND PUNISHABLE OFFENSE. DO NOT DO IT.

Stay Safe. Do Not Be Like Bob. Now Gerrarra Here!
